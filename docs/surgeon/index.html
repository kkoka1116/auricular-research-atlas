Costal cartilage for an auricular framework

An interactive explanation of the computational work: what was built, what the searches found, and which assumptions need surgical review.

Development studies through 18 September 2026 · Review prepared 28 September 2026

The question

Could a defined ear framework be divided into carveable pieces, allocated to available adult cartilage, and then formed and assembled into the intended shape?

The intended application is a future cadaveric-stock workflow. The present experiments use synthetic shapes and patient CT segmentations as anatomical proxies.

Current conclusion

A curved helix precursor fits in a selected adult eighth-cartilage example. Some other components have verified geometric placements. A complete four-component framework has not been allocated or physically validated; the supporting base remains unresolved. The tested deformation modes have not added fits beyond the updated rigid search.

01

From an ear surface to a construction proposal

The starting point was a published external-ear STL. After correcting an early extraction that lost faces, we retained the complete closed ear surface. We then drafted a base, helix, antihelix and tragal–antitragal unit against it.

What informed the pieces

The supplied dissection handbook and surgical literature describe construction roles and assembly. They do not provide calibrated three-dimensional cut coordinates for this ear. Our paths, thicknesses and attachment positions are engineering proposals.

For surgical review

Is this the right architecture?

The draft spans about 25.3 mm in the reference depth axis. The handbook mentions approximately 12 mm overall depth, but the definitions have not been reconciled. We should agree on the measurement convention and whether the base is unnecessarily contoured before interpreting a failed fit.

02

Recipient size and source age are separate inputs

The source STL did not establish its own physical units. We therefore declared the ear height explicitly and preserved the cartilage’s native CT millimeter scale.

Loading the reference ear…

What 58.9 mm means

It is a reported mean from unaffected ears in a unilateral-microtia cohort, used as a working benchmark. It is not an exact age-specific norm or a measured contralateral ear for a CCSeg case. The paper’s age reporting has an unresolved inconsistency.

The source-model paper describes an 11-year-old patient. Our earlier description of that STL as an adult ear was incorrect. Uniform benchmark scaling does not recover the patient’s original size.

The paired size experiment

With the earlier component design, both 58.9 and 61.5 mm targets allocated 2/4 units against the same ten adult cases: antihelix and tragus. Smaller ear height alone did not resolve the missing pieces.

First, check whether the measurements respond to known changes

A synthetic framework allowed controlled displacement, bending, thinning and loss of relief. Noise, a missing surface patch and reduced mesh resolution were treated as acquisition or processing artifacts.

A small global error can hide a clinically important local defect. Best-fit alignment can also reduce apparent distortion.

Controlled local displacement: 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 mm. These are software checks, not clinical detection thresholds.

What was measured and independently checked?

Surface RMSE, the 95th percentile, sampled Hausdorff distance with a conservative upper bound, predefined synthetic regions, construction-column thickness and valid closed-surface overlap. Plane distances, box volumes, translated overlap and known registration supplied independent analytic checks.

Thickness here is not general wall-normal cartilage thickness. Open or unreliable surfaces trigger unavailable-metric warnings. The tool does not yet establish clinical sensitivity, specificity or a surgeon-reviewed regional defect threshold.

On five deliberately constructed synthetic blanks, optimized placement fitted 3/5; fixed and bounding-box baselines each fitted 1/5. These are test fixtures, not anatomical cases or donor-match rates.

04

What fitted—and why the study stage matters

Early whole-framework and rigid-component searches were incomplete models of an assembled ear. Later iterations added source-age selection, connected cartilage, larger inventories and different component shapes. Their counts cannot be combined into one success rate.

What a verified fit means here

  1. A proposed cut has fixed dimensions and lies entirely inside the segmented cartilage.
  2. A separate geometric check verifies the research margin, currently 0.8 mm.
  3. Allocation must prevent different pieces from consuming the same stock. No artificial cartilage fills segmentation gaps.

A search that found no accepted candidate is not a proof of impossibility. Clearance is a research setting, not a clinical safety allowance.

Connected blocks and larger inventories

The expanded search included native connected regions and available labels on both sides. Four primary case identifiers had an accepted cut spanning multiple labels. A previously harvested, age-unverified case supplied three of four units in that historical search.

Cadaveric sourcing expands potential availability; it does not make every labeled region usable. A surgical block spanning an interchondral joint may include supporting tissues absent from a cartilage-only mask.

Kim et al., 2021: junction variability · Connected-stock results

Could pieces from multiple donors complete the framework?

We built a pooled allocation prototype that tracks accepted candidates and avoids overlapping use of the same stock. More donors can help only if each required component has at least one accepted candidate. In the expanded release-wide catalog, the current base remained unallocated. Repeating available pieces could not fill that gap.

The latest search found additional antihelix and tragal cuts, but those new cuts have not been jointly allocated. No complete multi-donor framework, tissue-bank workflow or clinical multi-donor feasibility was established.

What this experiment found

The additional search recovered rigid cuts: base 0/10, antihelix 3/10 and tragal 7/10. Allowing the four deformation modes added no verified case–component fits beyond that improved rigid control.

How to read the animation

The displayed states minimize a restricted elastic model. A guide can push and pull; a stitch pulls and can become slack. Attachment positions were proposed automatically, not taken from surgeon-approved landmarks. Neighboring parts do not deform in this model.

Removing all support returns a purely elastic model to its assumed stress-free shape. Playback has no time scale and does not predict tissue recovery, permanent set, suture pull-through or damage.

What was assumed

The central material scenario uses 8.8 MPa, with 5.9 and 11.7 MPa sensitivity scenarios informed by reported flexural testing. Transferring those values to these three-dimensional components is an engineering assumption, not specimen calibration.

The 5%, 10% and 20% sampled strain screens are exploratory. None is an established safe deformation limit. This is not a full finite-element model with coupled contacts.

Where the bending idea comes from—and what it does not establish

Clinical descriptions include bending an antihelix to form an antitragus and securing it to a base. That supports separating a starting cut from the assembled shape. It does not validate arbitrary folds and twists in our geometry. An ex vivo porcine assembly study also used electromechanical reshaping, which is different from the passive elastic mechanism modeled here.

Wang et al., 2025 · Gandy et al., 2016 · Full methods, constitutive assumptions and checks

The earlier helix experiment

An 84.957 mm curved strip fitted within one selected adult eighth-cartilage example. At 2.7 mm depth, 1.5 and 2.4 mm widths passed the geometric check; 3.4 mm was not certified. The example was selected after screening, so this is not a population success rate.

06

What would make the next experiment informative?

The most useful next step is to resolve the component design and measurement conventions, then test the model against carved tissue. This is a proposed research sequence for discussion.

Geometry

Review the base and depth

Should the support be shallower, divided and fastened, or integrated with the antihelix? Define clinically meaningful surfaces and the intended framework-depth measurement.

Construction

Identify usable stock and junctions

Confirm rib labels, cut landmarks, tissue bridges, preservation requirements and which larger cadaveric regions can reasonably be treated as usable blanks.

Mechanics

Define attachments and forming

Identify branch motions, guide contact, stitch locations and neighboring-piece deformation. Specify how shape error should be judged after guide removal.

Validation

Choose the first physical comparison

For a reviewed strip and one reviewed support component, record the carved free shape, section dimensions, tissue condition, force–displacement and shape after release or fixation.

What is not established

Clinical or manufacturing readiness, durable tissue behavior, safe strain, machining access and a complete allocated framework. The software’s 76-test development record checks numerical behavior; it does not supply clinical validation or independent human reproduction.

Interpretation and provenance

The reference ear comes from Díez-Montiel et al.’s 2024 supplementary models, adapted under CC BY-SA 4.0. Our modifications include reference extraction, normalization, drafted components and modeled deformation. The source authors did not provide the framework annotations.

This is a focused engineering review, not a clinical guideline or a systematic review. The handbook supplied construction concepts; its image is retained privately. Native CCSeg anatomy is also withheld from this public edition.
