Framework sizing and interpretation audit

17 September 2026. This audit follows the user's question about the apparent conflict between unsuccessful computational placements and established costal-cartilage reconstruction.

Correction to the interpretation

The current experiment does not test whether the selected anatomy can provide a surgically appropriate reconstructed ear. It tests whether an unvalidated, one-piece synthetic relief solid fits rigidly inside each selected label region. Applying the whole target to individual eighth and ninth cartilages is a software stress test; it does not test their usual contribution as separate framework components. The reported 18 pairs are not 18 failed surgical reconstructions.

The generator's nominal 3 mm relief amplitude is sampled on its radial mesh, so the actual maximum depth is 4.95 mm rather than the nominal base-plus-amplitude sum of 5.4 mm. The displayed audit uses the measured mesh dimensions. All scans were tested against the same target. A plausible external-ear height would still not validate this shape, solid footprint, thickness distribution or amount of cartilage needed for its construction.

Scaling audit

All three source NIfTI headers specify millimetres. Their affine voxel spacings are 0.548828 × 0.548828 × 0.5, 0.683594 × 0.683594 × 0.5, and 0.585938 × 0.585938 × 0.5 mm. The affines in the report agree with the original files.

For labels 6 and 8 in each scan, exported PLY bounds were independently compared with native occupied-voxel bounds expanded by half a voxel and transformed through the source affine. All six comparisons agreed within 0.001 mm. These checks found no header-to-export scale discrepancy. They do not independently validate scanner calibration, segmentation accuracy or clinical target size. Numerical details are retained in output/ccseg/sizing-audit.json.

How surgery differs

The 2023 Sharma technique report describes a base fashioned from seventh and part of sixth cartilage, with other features made from separate cartilage portions and assembled. A connected base harvested together is not the same proposition as carving every feature from that base without assembly.

A 56-patient report on modified helix/antihelix fabrication describes dividing cartilage into parts and changing component allocation according to available stock. This supports the user's point that whole or partial segments can contribute to different features; allocation is technique- and anatomy-dependent.

En-bloc cadaveric framework milling has also been demonstrated. Our three development scans and arbitrary target do not reproduce that paper's target, stock or fabrication conditions, so our result neither confirms nor refutes its feasibility finding.

Consequences for the build

The viewer now calls this the whole-shape test, displays actual target dimensions and explicitly states that clinical reconstruction feasibility has not been evaluated. Numerical results are retained unchanged; resizing the target simply to obtain a passing result would not resolve the modeling issue.

Before making a surgical fit claim, establish recipient-based target dimensions, an editable surgically reviewed framework and an explicit architecture. For assembled frameworks, allocate actual base, helix, antihelix and other component geometries across available stock without overlap, and define any permitted reshaping and joints. For en-bloc manufacture, use the intended whole framework and an appropriate physical blank definition. Compare those tasks separately. Finite search and a conservative verifier are additional reasons that 'not certified' cannot establish impossibility.
